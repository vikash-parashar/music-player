Queen Esther: For Such a Time as This

In the days of the great Persian Empire, there reigned a king named Ahasuerus. His kingdom stretched from India all the way to Ethiopia, across one hundred and twenty seven provinces.

One day, the king's queen, Vashti, refused to come when he called for her. The king was angry, and he decided to choose a new queen. So the most beautiful young women from every province were brought to the palace.

Among them was a young Jewish woman named Hadassah, though most people knew her by her Persian name, Esther. She was an orphan. Her parents had died, and she had been raised by her older cousin, Mordecai, who loved her as his own daughter. Before Esther went to the palace, he gave her a careful piece of advice. Do not tell anyone that you are Jewish.

Esther was gentle and wise, and everyone who saw her liked her. And when she was brought before the king, he loved her more than all the others. He set the royal crown upon her head and made her queen.

Not long after, Mordecai was sitting at the king's gate when he overheard two guards plotting to kill the king. He told Esther, and Esther warned the king.

Now there was a powerful man in the kingdom named Haman. The king promoted him above all the other princes and commanded that everyone bow down to him. And everyone did. Everyone except Mordecai.

When Haman learned that Mordecai was a Jew, his pride turned into a terrible hatred. It was not enough to punish Mordecai alone. Haman decided to destroy all the Jewish people in the whole kingdom.

He went to the king and told him there was a certain people scattered throughout his provinces who did not keep the king's laws. It is not for the king's profit to suffer them, Haman said. And he offered ten thousand talents of silver to pay for their destruction. The king gave Haman his ring and let him do as he pleased. A decree was sent out to every province. On a certain day, the Jewish people were to be destroyed.

When Mordecai heard, he tore his clothes and put on sackcloth and ashes, and he cried with a loud and bitter cry.

Mordecai sent a message to Esther, begging her to go to the king and plead for her people. But Esther was afraid. There was a law that anyone who went to the king without being called could be put to death, unless the king held out his golden sceptre to them. And the king had not called for her in thirty days.

Mordecai sent back a reply that has echoed down through the ages. Do not think that you will escape just because you live in the palace. If you stay silent now, help will come from another place. And who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?

For such a time as this.

Esther made her decision. She asked Mordecai to gather all the Jews in the city and fast for her for three days, and she and her maidens would fast too. Then, she said, I will go in unto the king, which is not according to the law. And if I perish, I perish.

On the third day, Esther put on her royal robes and walked to the inner court of the palace. The king looked up from his throne and saw her standing there.

And he held out the golden sceptre.

Esther came forward and touched the tip of it. What wilt thou, queen Esther, the king asked. It shall be even given thee to the half of the kingdom.

Esther simply invited the king and Haman to a banquet. Haman was delighted. But on his way home, he saw Mordecai at the gate, still refusing to bow, and he ordered a tall gallows to be built for him.

That very night, the king could not sleep. He asked for the royal records to be read to him. And there he heard how Mordecai had once saved his life. What honor has been done for this man, the king asked. Nothing, his servants said. So the next morning, the king commanded Haman himself to dress Mordecai in royal robes and lead him through the city on the king's own horse.

At the second banquet, the king asked Esther again what she wanted. And now, at last, Esther spoke. Let my life be given me, and my people. For we are sold, I and my people, to be destroyed.

The king was astonished. Who is he, and where is he, that durst presume in his heart to do so?

Esther pointed. The adversary and enemy is this wicked Haman.

Haman's plot was undone. The king gave Mordecai a place of honor, and a new decree allowed the Jewish people to defend themselves. Their sorrow was turned to joy. And to this day, that rescue is remembered with feasting and gladness at the festival of Purim.

And so our journey through these stories comes to an end, from the first light of creation to a brave queen who spoke up for her people. Thank you for listening.
