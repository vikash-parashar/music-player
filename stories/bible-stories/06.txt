The Exodus and the Parting of the Red Sea

Moses and his brother Aaron walked into the great palace of Egypt.

Thus saith the Lord God of Israel, Let my people go.

Pharaoh laughed. Who is the Lord, he said, that I should obey his voice? I know not the Lord, neither will I let Israel go.

And instead of setting the slaves free, he made their work even harder. No longer would they be given straw to make bricks. They would have to gather it themselves, and still make just as many bricks as before. The people groaned, and some of them blamed Moses for making things worse.

But God told Moses not to lose heart. Now shalt thou see what I will do to Pharaoh.

So began the plagues of Egypt. Moses stretched out his staff over the river Nile, and the water turned to blood. The fish died, and no one could drink. But Pharaoh's heart was hard, and he would not listen.

Then came frogs. Thousands of them, hopping out of the river and into houses, into beds, into ovens and kneading bowls.

Then came lice, crawling over people and animals. Then swarms of flies. Then a sickness that struck the cattle of Egypt. Then painful boils. Then a mighty hailstorm, with fire running along the ground. Then locusts, so many that they covered the face of the earth and ate every green thing that was left. Then darkness, a thick darkness that could be felt, for three whole days. Yet in the land of Goshen, where the Israelites lived, there was light.

After each plague, Pharaoh would bend a little. And then he would harden his heart once more.

At last, God told Moses that one final plague would come, the most terrible of all. At midnight, the firstborn in every house in Egypt would die. But the Israelites would be protected. Each family was to take a young lamb and mark the doorposts of their home with its blood. Then they were to eat a special meal, roasted lamb with bitter herbs and bread made without yeast.

When I see the blood, God said, I will pass over you.

That night, a great cry rose up across Egypt. But in every house marked with the blood of the lamb, the children were safe. And from that day to this, that night has been remembered as the Passover.

Before dawn, Pharaoh sent for Moses and Aaron. Rise up, he said, and get you forth from among my people. Go.

And so they went. Hundreds of thousands of people, men and women and children, with their flocks and herds, poured out of Egypt. After four hundred and thirty years, they were free. God went before them, by day in a pillar of cloud to show them the way, and by night in a pillar of fire to give them light.

But in his palace, Pharaoh began to regret what he had done. Who would build his cities now? So he gathered his army, six hundred of his finest chariots, with horses and riders and captains, and raced after them.

The Israelites had camped beside the Red Sea. And when they looked back and saw the dust of Pharaoh's army rising on the horizon, they were terrified. In front of them was deep water. Behind them were the chariots of Egypt. There was nowhere to go.

They cried out to Moses in despair. But Moses stood firm. Fear ye not, he said. Stand still, and see the salvation of the Lord. The Lord shall fight for you, and ye shall hold your peace.

Then the pillar of cloud moved from in front of the people to behind them. It stood between Israel and the Egyptian army.

And God said to Moses, Lift thou up thy rod, and stretch out thine hand over the sea, and divide it.

Moses raised his staff over the water. And a strong east wind began to blow. All night long it blew, and the sea began to move. The waters pulled back and rose up on either side like great walls, and in the middle, the floor of the sea became dry ground.

The children of Israel walked right through the middle of the sea. On their right and on their left, the water stood tall and shimmering.

The Egyptians followed them into the sea. But their chariot wheels clogged and dragged in the mud. And when every Israelite was safely on the far shore, Moses stretched out his hand again. The walls of water came crashing down, and the army of Pharaoh was gone.

On the far shore, the people sang for joy. Miriam, the sister of Moses, took up a tambourine, and all the women followed her, dancing and singing. Sing ye to the Lord, for he hath triumphed gloriously.

They were free at last. Ahead lay a long journey through the wilderness, and many years later, a land of their own. And in that land, generations later, a shepherd boy would face a giant.
